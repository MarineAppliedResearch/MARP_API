Where the months go

Recording it is the fast part.

A vehicle records for as long as it has power. Watching that back and working out what is in it has never scaled with the cameras — and the watching is not even where most of the time goes. It goes into everything between the stages.

Every step rebuilds the last one's context

Video, navigation and observations get separated when they move between tools, then joined back up by hand on the other side. Putting them back together is not the science. It is the toll for doing the science.

Nothing starts until the step before it finishes

Review waits on processing, analysis waits on review, and the report waits on all of it. A stage sits idle not because there is nothing to do, but because the batch it needs is not finished.

The answers arrive after the questions moved on

A survey goes out to answer something — how much is out there, whether it is changing, what this place looks like now. The longer the answers take, the less of that they can still affect.

What MARP changes

The same stages, no longer standing in line.

MARP does not make any of this work disappear. It keeps every stage attached to the same record, so a stage can start on what is ready instead of waiting for the one before it to finish everything.

All of that rests on one thing: there is a single record underneath. The video, where the vehicle was, what a model proposed, what a biologist decided and what got corrected are the same data throughout. Nothing has to be rebuilt to move forward, and a correction made during review is the version everything downstream reads.

Why the workflow is narrow on purpose

A system that can do anything has to be told everything.

Software built to support any scientific process has to ask. Which files belong together. What this column means. Which step comes after this one. What the finished thing is supposed to look like. Every project answers those questions again, and the answers end up in somebody's head or in a spreadsheet beside the data.

MARP is built around one sequence of work, so most of them are already answered. It knows video belongs to a dive and a dive belongs to a project. It knows a detection is a proposal until a biologist has looked at it. It knows what a reviewed observation is for. That is what lets the interface stay small — it can fill things in, hand one stage to the next, and otherwise stay out of the way.

The narrowness is the point, and it stops at the surface. Underneath are ordinary pieces — one API, one data model, services that do one job each — and those are reusable anywhere. The workflow sitting on top of them is deliberately not.

Who decides

Biologists lead. MARP amplifies.

A model can look at every frame of a survey, which no person has the hours to do. It cannot tell you whether what it found matters, and it is wrong often enough that treating its output as data would poison the record. So a detection is a proposal until a biologist accepts it, and the acceptance is recorded as that person's.

A detection is a proposal

Models put candidates in front of a reviewer. Nothing a model produced becomes an observation on its own.

The decision has a name on it

MARP records who reviewed what and what they decided, and keeps the earlier decisions instead of writing over them.

A correction goes upstream

Fixing an identification changes the record everything else reads, not a copy of it inside one application.

The dull half is the automated half

Pulling frames, matching timecodes, moving files, assembling the page in front of the reviewer. None of it needs a scientist.
